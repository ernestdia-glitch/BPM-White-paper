Perspective

Why business process management matters, and why it needs governance led by business process owners

Strategy describes what a company wants to achieve. Processes are how it actually happens, every day, in every site and every function. A company that does not manage its processes deliberately is managing them by accident — and the arrival of artificial intelligence inside those processes makes ownership more, not less, necessary.

Ernesto Diaz

Global Quality & Operations, Neuchâtel, Switzerland.

Published online: 16 September 2026

Abstract

Every organization runs on cross-functional processes, yet most process management programmes stall once the documentation is done: nobody owns the process end to end. This Perspective argues that the missing element is not method or technology but governance, and that its pivot is the business process owner (BPO) — a single accountable person who, coordinating a multidisciplinary team with the proper expertise, designs the standard way of working, ensures the people who operate it are competent, monitors whether it delivers, and drives improvement across every function and site.

We propose a practical set of gates for deciding which processes warrant a dedicated BPO and which do not.

Finally, we examine how artificial intelligence transforms each of the BPO's responsibilities. Regulatory evidence — including the first FDA warning letter citing over-reliance on AI — can be interpreted as supporting the same conclusion: we argue that the standard that steers an AI agent should remain human-authored and human-owned, and that anything that must never happen belongs in a deterministic control, not an instruction.

Key points

  • A business process is defined by the boundaries it crosses; functional excellence does not produce process excellence, because performance is lost at the hand-offs.
  • Mapping is not managing. Governance is the most frequently skipped of the six core elements of BPM maturity, and its absence turns process programmes into documentation programmes.
  • The four-tier governance model proposed here — process council, BPO, local process managers, experts and system owners — is designed to assign end-to-end accountability without dissolving functional authority.
  • In the model presented here, only cross-functional, multi-site, tool-supported, measurable processes justify a dedicated BPO; others need a framework owner, a functional head or no owner at all.

What a business process really is

Every organization runs on processes, whether or not anyone has ever drawn them. A business process is a set of linked activities that takes an input, adds value to it and delivers an outcome that matters to a customer, internal or external. Its defining characteristic is that it crosses boundaries. An order-to-cash process passes through sales, logistics, finance and customer service; a change-control process passes through every function that the change touches — technical, operational, regulatory, commercial. No single department ever sees the whole of it.

This is why processes so often perform badly even when every department performs well. Each function optimizes its own step; nobody optimizes the flow. Companies are organized around tasks and functions, while customers experience outcomes and end-to-end flows1.

Business process management (BPM) is the discipline that grew out of that thinking. The Association of Business Process Management Professionals describes BPM as a disciplined approach to identify, design, execute, document, measure, monitor and control processes, automated and manual alike, so that they deliver consistent, targeted results aligned with strategy2. Two words in that definition carry the weight: consistent and aligned. They are what separate BPM from a one-off improvement project. Dumas and colleagues put it more plainly: BPM is the art and science of overseeing how work is performed so that outcomes are reliable and opportunities for improvement are seized3.

Why BPM matters to a company

Mapping is not managing

The most common reason BPM initiatives stall has nothing to do with method. The process maps are produced, the procedures are written, the training is delivered, and then the process goes back to being nobody's responsibility. Functional heads own their functions. The general manager owns everything and therefore, in practice, no single process. The consequences are predictable: local variants multiply from site to site, indicators are reported but not acted on, and an improvement made in one place is never carried to the others.

The Handbook on Business Process Management identifies six core elements of a mature BPM capability: strategic alignment, governance, methods, information technology, people and culture5 (Fig. 2). Governance — who decides, who owns, who is accountable — is the element most often skipped, and its absence is what quietly turns a process programme into a documentation programme. This paper argues that ownership has to be explicit and end to end, and that this is the role of the business process owner.

A governance structure led by business process owners

The governance model proposed here has four tiers (Fig. 3). In our view the number of layers matters less than the clarity of what each one decides. The process council sets priorities across processes, resolves conflicts between a process standard and a functional or site interest, and allocates resources; without it, the BPO has nowhere to escalate and no mandate behind the standard. The BPO holds end-to-end accountability for one process, or a small family of closely related processes, across all functions and all sites. Local process managers, often called key users, own execution and adherence at site or function level and are the first line of feedback to the BPO. Subject-matter experts and system owners provide technical depth, configure the supporting tool and safeguard the data; they serve the process but do not own it.

The four responsibilities of the BPO

In the model presented here, the BPO's mandate is stated in four verbs — design, train, monitor, improve — each with what the owner owns, how it is measured and how it characteristically fails (Table 1). Three points deserve emphasis beyond the table. Design includes deciding explicitly what is mandatory everywhere and where local flexibility is allowed; an undocumented boundary is renegotiated at every site. Monitoring that reaches no decision forum, or a community session without follow-up, is measurement for its own sake. Improvement is where the role earns its cost: the BPO is why a lesson learned in one site becomes the standard in all, so that improvement compounds rather than fragments.

AI as advisor and analyst is a fifth capability alongside the human experts. An agent can surface patterns in deviation data, draft risk assessments, flag control-chart anomalies and pre-screen change requests; decision authority stays with the experts who evaluate, challenge and approve. The proposed governance model assumes that the governance structure defines where AI outputs enter the workflow, which review gates they pass, and how the team records its rationale when it accepts or overrides a recommendation.

Box 1 | What a BPO needs in order to succeed

A title is not a mandate. We propose that, to work, the role needs six things:

  1. A defined scope with clear interfaces to adjacent processes, so that two owners never dispute the same hand-off.
  2. Authority over the standard, even without authority over the people, with an agreed escalation path when a site or function departs from it.
  3. A seat in management review, so that performance and non-adherence reach the people who can act.
  4. Ownership of, or at minimum a decisive voice on, the system that supports the process. An owner who cannot influence the tool configuration cannot enforce the design.
  5. Three to five indicators they are personally accountable for, and no more.
  6. Protected time. A BPO role added on top of a full functional job loses to the functional job every week.

Whether a given process warrants the role is decided by the gates in Box 2.

Box 2 | Four gates before appointing a BPO

The gates are our adaptation of the governance criteria of the BPM CBOK2, with a compliance overlay for regulated industries. They are walked in order, not tallied: a no at Gate 1 ends the analysis without an owner, a yes at Gate 2 ends it with one, and Gate 4 decides what kind.

  1. Gate 1 — Does it cross functions and sites? Activity confined to one department, or to one site, is adequately run by its functional manager. A BPO's value lies in resolving hand-offs and accountability gaps between departments and in removing variation between sites; without that friction there is no need for a separate owner.
  2. Gate 2 — Is it regulated or audited? If an external body — auditor, notified body, health authority — can hold the company accountable for the process, ambiguous ownership is a liability in itself. A yes here settles that the process needs a named, accountable owner regardless of Gate 3; go to Gate 4.
  3. Gate 3 — Is it strategic or high-risk? Customer-facing impact, chronic performance problems, a candidate for major investment or automation, or variation between sites that creates real cost in compliance, efficiency or customer outcome. If none applies, a formal owner is premature; periodic review is enough until the risk profile changes.
  4. Gate 4 — Is there something to own? A common workflow or system that every instance follows, and three to five meaningful indicators that describe it. Both present: appoint a BPO. A method applied inside other processes but no single workflow: appoint a framework owner (Table 5). Neither: the process belongs to a functional head or, where the law places it, to a regulatory role.

The BPO and the functional manager

The BPO is never appointed into an empty space: each already has a functional manager who runs most of it and reasonably regards it as theirs — the Supplier Quality Manager for supplier qualification, the Learning & Development Manager for training, the QA Compliance lead for deviations and CAPA. In the model presented here, the function is the unit of execution and the process is the unit of design. The functional manager is accountable for the function's contribution to the process — the people, the budget and the quality of the work done there; the BPO is accountable for the process's contribution to the organization — its standard, its end-to-end indicators, its hand-offs and its improvement. Line power stays with the function, which is why the six conditions in Box 1 exist. We propose resolving the overlap by writing three lines into the process charter: the BPO owns the standard, the hand-offs and the system rules, and any change to them passes through the BPO; the functional manager owns how the work is done inside the function; when a departmental indicator conflicts with the end-to-end one, the process indicator wins. In mid-size organizations one person may hold both roles, provided the charter says which decisions are made in which capacity and a counterpart outside the reporting line exists; in large multi-site organizations the BPO is drawn from outside the dominant function and functional managers act as process stewards at each site. Named and bounded, the overlap gives the process a hand that executes and a hand that designs; denied, it gives the process two owners and therefore none.

The proposed model is simple to draw and easy to get wrong. The recurring failure modes, their symptoms and their remedies are summarized in Table 2. The common thread is that each failure removes one of the six conditions in Box 1 while leaving the organization chart intact, so the structure looks complete on paper for months before the absence of ownership shows in the indicators.

How artificial intelligence changes the practice of BPM

For thirty years BPM has been practised largely by hand: processes discovered through interviews and workshops, documented in diagrams and procedures, monitored through indicators compiled after the fact, and improved through projects. Artificial intelligence does not change what BPM is for, but we argue that it changes every one of the four verbs in the BPO's mandate (Table 3), and that it raises rather than lowers the need for an owner.

Design, from interviews to evidence. Process mining reconstructs the process as it actually runs from the event logs already sitting in the enterprise, workflow and transaction systems the process runs on6. Instead of asking people how a case moves from opening to closure, the BPO can see every variant that has occurred, where work waits, where it loops back and which sites diverge from the standard. The as-is workshop becomes a fact base. Generative models then do the drafting — process models, procedures, work instructions, training material, a first impact assessment — and can simulate how a redesigned flow would behave before anyone changes it. When a process-mining model is kept continuously synchronized with live execution data, it becomes a digital twin of the process: a virtual replica that the BPO can use to test "what if" scenarios — a new control point, a different approval sequence, a site consolidation — and predict the impact on cycle time, risk and resource load before committing the change to the real process.

Train, from courses to expertise anchored in practice. The persistent gap between "read and understood" and actual behaviour has a plausible explanation in how memory works: a procedure read on screen enters short-term memory, which holds little and fades within hours unless the content is retrieved and used; it becomes long-term, procedural memory — the kind that shapes what people actually do under pressure — only through repeated, spaced practice in context. If this model holds, a conventional LMS, optimized for delivery and acknowledgement, is addressing only the first step. The workforce could be trained on paper without having anchored the practice, and the way of working would then rest on individual recall rather than embedded competence. AI-enabled approaches could shift the emphasis. Assistants answering questions from controlled procedures at the moment of need — on the shop floor, at the desk, inside the workflow system — might close part of that gap, because each retrieval at the point of use is one more repetition that moves the procedure from short-term recall to long-term habit. Simulation of high-consequence decisions — an escalation, a change classification, a customer-facing decision — could reinforce the right response without real-world risk. Error patterns, linked to teams and roles, might allow targeted requalification rather than annual retraining of everyone. If this direction proves sound, the BPO's role would shift from ensuring that training records are complete to ensuring that expertise is genuinely anchored — measuring not whether people attended, but whether behaviour changed and whether the culture of doing things right the first time is strengthening.

Monitor, from lagging indicators to prediction. Conformance checking compares live execution against the designed model continuously, so drift is detected in days rather than at the next review. Anomaly detection on cycle-time, error and customer-feedback data surfaces trends before a threshold is breached; predictive models flag which actions are likely to miss their date, which changes carry unusual risk and which partners are heading for trouble. A management review can put a question to the process data during the meeting rather than to a slide prepared three weeks earlier, and the periodic performance review becomes largely assembled by the system and reviewed by people. The full power of this shift emerges when the process digital twin introduced at the design stage is fed by interconnected systems and databases — enterprise resource planning, execution, workflow and partner systems — creating a single, continuously updated data layer. With that layer in place, monitoring is no longer a periodic report compiled from siloed sources; it is a live, cross-system view of process health that the BPO can query, drill into and act on in real time.

Improve, from projects to continuous. Clustering similar incidents across sites and years exposes recurrence that no single investigator could see, which is the single largest opportunity in most corrective-action systems. Models propose root-cause hypotheses and improvement options for the owner to test, and agentic systems can execute the routine, low-risk steps of a workflow — routing, classification, completeness checks, drafting a case summary from the record — while a person approves. Improvement stops being an annual initiative and becomes a standing capability of the process.

What AI does not change: who is accountable (a life-sciences angle)

Regulators have been unusually quick and unusually clear on this point; the most detailed body of rules so far comes from the pharmaceutical sector, but the expectation it expresses is general (Table 4). The draft Annex 22 to the EU GMP guide, developed with PIC/S, requires that any AI used in GMP be fit for a defined intended use, validated, traceable in its training and test data and subject to human oversight, and it excludes adaptive and generative models from GMP-critical decisions altogether7. The FDA and EMA jointly published ten guiding principles for AI practice built around human-centred design, a risk-based approach and a clear context of use8; the ISPE GAMP guide on AI and the FDA's computer software assurance guidance supply the validation framework9,10; and the EU AI Act's obligations for high-risk systems, which include AI used in quality and process control, began phasing in from August 202611. These requirements can be interpreted as converging on one expectation: that the organization's management system, and therefore a named owner, remains in control of how AI is used.

Box 3 | The first warning letter for over-reliance on AI

In April 2026 the FDA issued what is regarded as the first warning letter to cite over-reliance on artificial intelligence in drug manufacturing12. Investigators found that the manufacturer had used AI agents to generate product specifications, procedures and master production records without the quality-unit review that cGMP requires. Asked why a required validation step had not been performed, the firm answered that the AI had not told them it was necessary. The regulator did not accept that answer.

The case is instructive precisely because nothing about it was exotic. The tools worked as designed; what was missing was an owner who had decided, in advance, what the tools were permitted to produce, what a human had to review before it became a controlled record, and how the resulting documents would enter document control and change control. A possible implication is that those are process-design decisions, and that a process owner is the natural role to take them.

A worked example: applying the gates to quality processes

The gates in Box 2 are easiest to see applied to one family of processes. The quality management system of a regulated manufacturer (e.g. pharmaceuticals, medical devices, food) is a convenient example because its processes are well defined, comparable across companies and inspected by third parties, and because it contains every ownership pattern the model produces. Not every process needs an owner of this kind, and creating owners indiscriminately produces committees rather than accountability; the gates decide the matter. In a regulated environment every core quality process passes Gate 2 — each is inspected — so the decision turns on Gates 1, 3 and 4. The processes that, in our assessment, pass all three and therefore justify a dedicated BPO are deviation and CAPA management, change control, document control, training and qualification, supplier qualification, customer complaints and feedback, and crisis management and post-market action. Processes that pass Gates 1 and 3 but not Gate 4 — quality risk management, validation, management review, internal audit — have a method to own but no single workflow, and need a framework owner rather than an owner of execution. Processes that fail Gate 1 because they are site-bound and technical belong to a functional head, and processes that are in fact regulatory roles, such as batch certification, should not be given an owner at all (Table 5).
